What the book says

Equilibrium ends the mind-reading

Christian and Griffiths describe game theory as a problem of recursion: I think about what you think about what I think, with no natural stopping point. An equilibrium cuts the loop. It's a set of strategies that each player would keep even knowing the other's, so nobody needs to out-think anyone.

Existing isn't the same as findable

John Nash proved every finite game has at least one equilibrium. The authors point out that computer scientists later showed finding one can be computationally intractable, which casts doubt on equilibrium as a prediction of how real players, with limited time, will behave.

Stable doesn't mean good

In the prisoner's dilemma, each player's best move is to defect no matter what the other does. Both follow that logic and land in the one cell that's worse for both than mutual cooperation. That's the gap between the Nash outcome and the Pareto outcome, and the price of anarchy puts a number on it. The book notes the price varies a lot by system: for selfish drivers routing through traffic, it's been shown to be surprisingly modest, while for a dilemma like this one it can be steep.

The race to the bottom

The book applies the same structure to the tragedy of the commons and to unlimited vacation policies. If time off is uncapped, whoever takes a little less looks a little more committed, so everyone ratchets down, and the equilibrium can be less vacation than a fixed allowance would have given. That's the second story in this lab.

Change the game, not the players

Rather than hoping people act nobly, mechanism design reshapes the payoffs so that selfish play produces a good result. The authors use the mafia as a dark example: when informants face certain punishment from the boss, staying silent becomes the rational move, and both suspects are better off. They also suggest emotions like guilt, loyalty and the urge for revenge work as evolution's built-in mechanism design, making promises and threats credible.
